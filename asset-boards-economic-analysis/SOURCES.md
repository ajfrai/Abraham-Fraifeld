# Sources

Citations gathered across five research tracks and adversarially fact-checked.
Each is tagged with a **confidence** level and, where relevant, **contradicting evidence**.
Confidence reflects identification quality, not just journal prestige.

Legend: **[S]** strong · **[M]** moderate · **[W]** weak / suggestive · **[alleg]** allegation, not adjudicated.

## 1. Latent-demand revelation & aggregation theory

1. **[S]** Easley & Kleinberg, *Networks, Crowds, and Markets*, ch. 9 (Auctions) — order statistics; E[2nd-highest of *n* uniform] = (*n*−1)/(*n*+1), increasing in *n*. https://www.cs.cornell.edu/home/kleinber/networks-book/networks-book-ch09.pdf
2. **[S]** Auction theory / Revenue Equivalence (Vickrey 1961; Riley–Samuelson 1981; Myerson 1981): more bidders → higher expected revenue. Breaks under risk aversion / affiliated values / asymmetry. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auction_theory
3. **[S]** Royal Swedish Academy (2010), "Markets with Search Frictions" (Nobel background) — **Diamond paradox** (any positive search cost → monopoly price); congestion vs. thick-market externalities; Hosios condition. https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/advanced-economicsciences2010.pdf
4. **[M]** Stahl (1989), "Oligopolistic Pricing with Sequential Consumer Search," *AER* — expected price can *rise* as search cost falls / store count rises. https://www.jstor.org/stable/1827927
5. **[S]** Hosios (1990), *Rev. Econ. Stud.* 57:279–298 — efficiency knife-edge for search externalities.
6. **[S]** Bakos (1997), "Reducing Buyer Search Costs," *Management Science* 43:1676–1692 — lower buyer search costs *reduce* seller market power (contra naive thesis). https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.43.12.1676
7. **[S]** Engel & Rogers (1996), "How Wide Is the Border?" *AER* — market integration raises price in the surplus region. Survey: https://eml.berkeley.edu/~webfac/olney/e211_fa03/e211-jacks.pdf
8. **[S]** Roth (2008), "What Have We Learned from Market Design?" *Economic Journal* / NBER w13530 — thickness vs. congestion; thickness raises *match quality*, not inherently price. https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w13530/w13530.pdf
9. **[M]** Brynjolfsson & Smith (2000) / "Do lower search costs reduce prices and price dispersion?" — dispersion persists online; net price effect mixed. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167624504000307
10. **[S]** Loberto, Luciani & Pangallo (2020), Banca d'Italia — online listing interest (page-views) predicts shorter time-on-market / lower downward revisions; demand proxy. https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.02706
11. **[M]** Goldfarb & Tucker, "Digital Economics," NBER. https://conference.nber.org/confer/2019/DTs19/GoldfarbTucker.pdf
12. **[M]** Farronato et al. (2025), "Digital Platforms as Information Aggregators." https://conference.nber.org/confer/2025/DTs25/farronato.pdf
13. **[ref]** "Latent demand" definition (marketing term, not a theorem). https://www.tutor2u.net/economics/topics/latent-demand

### Reliability notes
- Peer-reviewed / Fed / NBER items (most of §1–§5) are cited with confidence.
- Industry/vendor/press items (#40–#42, take-rate figures) are **suggestive**, not causal.
- Famous-but-contested results (#49 Kerala, #52 jam, #61 Hsieh–Moretti) cited as "influential but contested."
- RealPage harm figures (#24–#25) are the strongest "platforms raised prices" evidence but are **associational** and rest on **allegations** of coordination not yet adjudicated.
- A few primary PDFs would not parse via automated fetch; figures from those were verified against abstracts/secondary summaries and flagged where they remain unverified (#19, #28).
